The buyer's deposit is the cheapest thing in the deal

What a sauna costs the day you decide, not the day it heats

A used two-person electric heater, the box and stones only, closes around $500 on the local listings. It does not heat anything yet. Getting to the first session is where the deposit earns its name: an 8 kW electric heater on a 240-volt, 40-amp circuit wants a two-pole GFCI breaker, about 60 feet of 8-gauge THHN in conduit, and four to six hours of an electrician who is not you. The parts run $180 to $260. The labor, at $85 an hour, runs $340 to $510. Even calling the total $700, the wire is only 1.4 percent of the first-year bill.

The propane version trades copper for logistics. A used 20-pound tank closes around $40 and needs no electrician, but a 6 kW-equivalent burner draws about 4 pounds an hour, so a 20-pound tank gives roughly five hours of heat and an exchange costs $25. That is $5.00 a session in tanks versus $0.90 in current, and the gap compounds: a twelve-session protocol month pays $60 in exchanges against $10.80 on the meter. The electric line costs more once and less every session after.

The buyer's math is not the deposit, it is the fold-back. Locking the purchase the day the listing posts caps the time on market at about three weeks, and mispriced gear on the local listings cuts 11 percent a month. A $900 ask that sits becomes a $700 counter, then a $500 close; the heater alone would have cost $629 if the buyer had waited out the seller's patience. The deposit is the cheapest option on any deal you intend to close anyway.

This is the arithmetic behind the cost-per-session reviews on HackedSelf: what the gear costs the day you commit, what it costs per dose after that, and no enthusiasm before evidence. A protocol you can price to the day is a protocol you keep.
